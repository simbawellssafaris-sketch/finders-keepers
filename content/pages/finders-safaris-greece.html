Six thousand islands, the birthplace of democracy, and a caldera left behind by one of the largest volcanic eruptions in recorded history.

Greece is where a great deal of what the West thinks about itself was first argued out loud. The Acropolis is the obvious statement of it, but the Ancient Agora below is the more affecting place — an ordinary marketplace where Socrates walked and citizens invented the practice of governing themselves in public.

Then there is the sea. Around six thousand islands and islets, of which a couple of hundred are inhabited, each with its own architecture, food and temperament. Santorini sits on the rim of a flooded volcanic crater and produces mineral white wine from vines woven into low baskets against the wind. Mykonos is cosmopolitan and deliberately labyrinthine. Delos, uninhabited and sacred, was so holy that nobody was permitted to be born or to die there.

Featured Accommodation

Oia, Santorini

Canaves Oia Hotel — built into the volcano wall of the caldera

Canaves Oia is cut directly into the caldera cliff at the edge of Oia, which is both an architectural decision and a geological one — the suites occupy spaces carved into volcanic rock, with terraces hung out over a drop into the flooded crater.

It is one of the island’s most exclusive addresses and sits in the heart of Oia rather than out along the road, which keeps it clear of the cruise-tour traffic while leaving the restaurants and galleries walkable. The rooms are spacious and bright, with independent balconies and, in many cases, private pools.

Guests reliably single out two things: the caldera views, which are the best on the island, and the breakfast, which is regularly described as unbeatable. The staff have a habit of producing birthday cakes and balloons without being asked, which is the kind of detail that makes a hotel a favourite rather than a stay.
