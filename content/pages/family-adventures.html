Overview

Why a safari, and why now.

Children are only the age they are once.

Most family holidays are an exercise in parallel activity. Everyone is nominally on the same trip, and then the teenagers are on their phones, the youngest wants the pool, the grandparents want an early night, and the family is together in the same place without ever quite being together.

A safari is different for a structural reason: the good hours are fixed. Everybody gets up before light because that is when the animals move, and everybody is in the same vehicle looking at the same thing. There is no signal in most camps and very little to do between the two drives except talk to each other. Families who have not had a proper conversation in months find they have had four by Wednesday.

It works across ages in a way that few things do. A six-year-old and a seventy-year-old can both be entirely absorbed by the same lion. The child does not need to understand the conservation politics to be gripped, and the grandparent does not need the adrenaline to find it extraordinary.

Most of our family journeys are tailor-made, built around how many of you are travelling, what the ages are and what everybody actually wants out of the week. A family of four with young children needs a different route, different driving distances and a different daily rhythm from three generations travelling together, and we would rather build two different journeys than sell the same one twice.

The practical things that decide whether it works

The difference between a family safari that lands and one that does not is rarely the wildlife. It is whether the driving days are short enough, whether the rooms connect, whether there is a pool for the middle of the day, and whether the guide can talk to a nine-year-old without talking down to them.

Those are the things we plan around first. The lodges come second, and the itinerary is shaped by the youngest and the oldest person travelling rather than the average of the group.
